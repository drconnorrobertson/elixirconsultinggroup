How to Value a Small Business: Beyond the Multiple

May 4, 2026 By Connor Robertson

One of the most common questions I get from people looking to acquire a business is: How much should I pay? What's the right multiple? Is three times EBITDA reasonable? Four times? Five times?

These are the wrong questions to be asking.

Most people who approach valuation this way end up paying too much. They fixate on a multiple and then work backwards to justify that multiple. They tell themselves that a SaaS company "deserves" a higher multiple, or that a services business should be cheaper. They compare the business they're looking at to some other business they heard about. They let emotions and comparisons override logic.

Real valuation is much simpler than this. And once you understand how it actually works, you'll be able to make much better acquisition decisions.

What You're Actually Buying

Let me start with first principles. When you acquire a business, what are you actually buying? You're buying the future cash flows of that business. That's it. Not the assets. Not the revenue. The cash that the business will generate in the future.

This is an important distinction. Revenue isn't cash. A business could have $10 million in revenue but generate negative cash flow. Conversely, a business could have lower revenue but generate robust cash flow. You care about cash.

So the fundamental question you need to answer is this: How much cash will this business generate in the future? And how much of that cash can I keep?

That's your answer. That's your valuation.

The Components of a Real Valuation

To value a business properly, you need to understand four things:

Current Cash Flow. What is the business actually generating in cash right now? Not EBITDA. I don't like EBITDA. I like cash. How much money comes in the door, and how much leaves? What's the actual profit after you pay everything, including taxes and capital expenditures?

Start here. Get this number right. Most of the business valuations I review get this wrong. They include add-backs that aren't real. They exclude expenses that will continue. They adjust for things that won't actually adjust. Get a clear-eyed view of the actual cash the business generates today.

Growth Rate. Is the business growing? At what rate? Is it growing because of the market, or because of unique competitive advantages? Is growth sustainable, or will it slow down?

Here's the thing about growth: a growing business is worth more than a flat business. But not infinitely more. A business growing at 5% annually is worth more than a business with zero growth, but a business growing at 25% annually is worth less than the multiple would suggest if that growth can't be sustained.

Focus on sustainable growth. Not growth you think might happen. Growth that's actually happening based on the business fundamentals.

Margin Sustainability. Will the margins you see today continue in the future? Or will they deteriorate? This is critical.

I evaluated a business once that had 40% gross margins. Great, right? But digging deeper, I discovered that half of the business was dependent on a single customer, and that customer was evaluating a lower-cost provider. If that customer left, margins would collapse. The stated margins weren't real margins. They were statistical artifacts of customer concentration.

Ask yourself: what would happen to margins if the competitive environment changed? If costs increased? If the biggest customer left? Could the business maintain its margins?

Capital Efficiency. Does this business require significant capital expenditure to maintain its position, or is it relatively capital light? Can you run it with minimal working capital, or does it require constant investment?

A business that generates $1 million in annual cash flow but requires $500,000 in annual capital expenditure is worth less than a business that generates $1 million in cash flow and requires minimal capital. With the capital-light business, you actually keep the cash. With the capital-heavy business, you have to reinvest it.

Putting It Together: A Real Valuation Framework

Here's how I actually value a business. I don't use multiples. I use cash flow.

Step one: Calculate the actual normalized annual cash flow. This is the cash the business generates after all expenses, taxes, and capital expenditures.

Step two: Project that cash flow forward. If the business is growing, what do you expect it to grow at? Be conservative. If it's flat, project it as flat. If it's declining, project it as declining.

Step three: Discount that cash flow stream to the present using a discount rate that reflects the risk. What return do you need to justify this investment? If you're acquiring a stable, profitable, boring business, maybe 15% return. If you're acquiring a riskier, more volatile business, maybe 25% return.

Using this approach, you can calculate the present value of the business. That's your valuation.

Here's an example. Imagine you're looking at a small services business that generates $200,000 in annual cash flow. It's been flat for the last five years. You don't see much growth potential. It's a good business, but not a growth story.

If you assume flat cash flow of $200,000 annually, and you require a 15% return on your investment, what's the value? It's about $1.3 million. That means you'd be willing to pay up to $1.3 million for this business.

If someone is asking $2 million, don't pay it. The math doesn't work. You'd be overpaying significantly.

Now imagine the same business but growing at 10% annually. Same cash flow of $200,000 today, but growing. Suddenly the valuation is different. Higher growth means higher value. How much higher depends on how long you can sustain that growth.

Why Multiples Are Dangerous

Here's why I don't like multiples: they're a shortcut that can mislead you. Someone tells you that SaaS companies trade at five times EBITDA. So you apply a five times multiple to the business you're looking at, and you conclude the price is fair. But you don't know if this business will grow like typical SaaS companies. You don't know if its margins are sustainable. You don't know if it has customer concentration risk.

Multiples can be a useful data point. It's interesting to know what other businesses have sold for. But multiples shouldn't be your valuation framework. Multiples are descriptive, not prescriptive.

The businesses that trade at the highest multiples are the ones with strong, sustainable growth, strong margins, and low capital requirements. If your business doesn't have those characteristics, it shouldn't trade at those multiples.

The Bottom Line

Value a business based on the cash it generates, not based on what similar businesses have sold for. Understand the components of that cash flow. Understand the risks to that cash flow. Discount appropriately for those risks. And make your decision based on whether you're comfortable with the risk-adjusted return.

If you do this, you'll make better acquisition decisions. You'll overpay less often. And you'll build real wealth through acquisitions that actually deliver value.

The key to acquisition success isn't finding great deals. It's not overpaying for the deals you find. Start with a sound valuation framework, and everything else gets easier.
